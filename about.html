My work centers on a practical statistical problem: how to extract reliable information from imperfect data when decisions have real consequences. I am particularly interested in computational methods for settings where information is incomplete, important factors are unobserved, relationships are complex, and measurement is imperfect. These conditions are not exceptions to real-world analysis; they are often the conditions under which consequential decisions must be made.

My academic interests lie at the intersection of computational statistics, quantitative social science, and the study of human systems. I am especially interested in methodological problems involving uncertainty, latent structure, complex interactions, and heterogeneous data, as well as their application to human security, political violence and radicalization, humanitarian crises, climate risk, and emerging technologies. Across these domains, I am drawn to questions where conventional data and methods provide only part of the answer.

Professionally, I apply statistical and computational methods in support of the U.S. Mission to the United Nations. My work connects quantitative evidence with policy-relevant analysis, using data to better understand complex international issues and support diplomacy and decision-making. This experience has reinforced my interest in methods that are not only statistically rigorous, but also interpretable, reproducible, and useful under real operational constraints.

The common thread across my work is methodological: extracting defensible information from difficult data, distinguishing what the evidence supports from what remains uncertain, and communicating those findings clearly enough to inform consequential decisions. I am ultimately interested in statistics as both a way to understand complex human systems and a practical tool for making better decisions when information is limited, uncertainty is unavoidable, and the stakes are high.
